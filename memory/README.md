## Papers
Below are the papers to be read to complete the assignments.

#### [Recollection and Familiarity in Dense Hippocampal Amnesia: A Case Study](article8b.pdf)

This study investigates the memory profile of a patient with severe hippocampal amnesia (VC) to better understand the roles of recollection and familiarity in recognition memory. Using extensive neuroimaging and neuropsychological assessments, the authors showed that VC had selective bilateral hippocampal abnormalities while adjacent cortical areas were relatively preserved. Experimental memory tests revealed severe impairments in recollection and familiarity for verbal and topographical material, but surprisingly preserved recognition for unfamiliar human faces. The findings challenge theories that strictly separate recollection and familiarity into different medial temporal structures and instead suggest that the hippocampus contributes to both processes depending on the type of material being remembered.

#### [Imagination and False Memory Inductions: Investigating the Role of Process, Content and Source of Imaginations](article9.pdf)

This article explores how different forms of imaginal encoding influence false memory formation using the Deese–Roediger–McDermott (DRM) paradigm. Across three experiments, the authors manipulated whether imagery was generated deliberately or automatically, whether the scripts originated from the participant or another person, and whether images were isolated or integrated. The findings demonstrated that deliberate self-generated imagery reduced false memory rates compared to externally guided imagery, supporting the idea that distinctive encoding and effective source monitoring help distinguish imagined events from real experiences. The study contributes to broader debates about the reliability of imagination in therapeutic and legal settings.

##3# [Differential Effect of Right and Left Basal Ganglionic Infarctions on Procedural Learning](article10.pdf)

This study examined how ischemic lesions confined to either the right or left basal ganglia affect procedural learning abilities. Patients with unilateral basal ganglia infarctions and healthy controls completed a range of procedural learning tasks, including the Tower of Hanoi, Mirror Reading, and Porteus Mazes. Results revealed distinct patterns depending on lesion location: right-sided lesions impaired improvement across repeated learning trials, whereas left-sided lesions more strongly affected baseline performance and transfer to novel tasks. The authors concluded that procedural learning is not a single unified function and that different procedural tasks depend on distinct neural systems within the basal ganglia.

#### [Second-Order ‘Fear’ Conditioning in Humans: Persistence of CR2 Following Extinction of CR1](article1.pdf)

This paper investigates second-order fear conditioning in humans and whether conditioned responses acquired indirectly can survive extinction of the original conditioned stimulus. Participants first learned to associate a geometric shape with an aversive loud noise and then learned a second association involving a new shape paired with the original conditioned stimulus. The researchers found that second-order conditioned responses persisted even after extinction of the first-order response, particularly in participants who perceived they had no control over the aversive stimulus. These findings support associative learning theories derived from animal research and have important implications for understanding the persistence of fear responses in clinical anxiety disorders.

#### [Beyond the Information Given: Causal Models in Learning and Reasoning](article4.pdf)

This theoretical article argues that human and animal learning cannot be explained solely through observed statistical associations, as proposed by classical associative theories inspired by David Hume. Instead, the authors present causal-model theory, which proposes that learners construct structured mental representations distinguishing causes from effects. Through examples from psychology and animal learning research, the article demonstrates that both humans and rats reason about hidden causal structures rather than simple event covariation. The paper highlights how causal models allow individuals to differentiate genuine causal relationships from spurious correlations and to make accurate predictions, diagnoses, and interventions.

#### [Stronger Renewal in Human Fear Conditioning When Tested With an Acquisition Retrieval Cue Than With an Extinction Retrieval Cue](article5.pdf)

This study examined how retrieval cues influence the return of conditioned fear after extinction in humans. Participants underwent fear acquisition and extinction procedures in different contexts, after which they were tested in the original acquisition environment using either acquisition-related or extinction-related retrieval cues. The researchers found that fear renewal was significantly stronger when acquisition cues were present, indicating that contextual and retrieval cues strongly influence the recovery of extinguished fear responses. The findings provide valuable insights into relapse mechanisms in anxiety disorders and suggest that extinction-based therapies may benefit from incorporating strategies that generalize extinction learning across contexts.

#### [Verbal and Spatial Working Memory in Autism](article6.pdf)

This article investigates whether individuals with high-functioning autism show impairments in verbal and spatial working memory. Using both standardized tests and experimental N-back tasks, the researchers compared children, adolescents, and adults with autism to matched control participants. The study found intact verbal working memory performance but significant deficits in spatial working memory among individuals with autism. The authors argue that working memory difficulties in autism emerge particularly when tasks involve increased information-processing demands, supporting theories emphasizing atypical neural connectivity and difficulties with complex cognitive integration rather than generalized memory deficits.

#### [Independent Representation of Parts and the Relations Between Them: Evidence From Integrative Agnosia](article7.pdf)

This study examines whether object recognition depends on independently representing object parts and the spatial relationships between them. The authors investigated a patient with integrative agnosia (S.M.), a disorder characterized by impaired integration of visual features into coherent wholes. Although S.M. could learn to recognize objects based on their individual parts, he struggled profoundly when objects contained the same parts arranged differently. The findings support structural description theories of object perception, suggesting that recognizing object components and encoding the spatial relations between them rely on separable cognitive mechanisms.

#### [Fractionation of Memory in Medial Temporal Lobe Amnesia](article8a.pdf)

This paper presents a detailed neuropsychological investigation of two patients with medial temporal lobe amnesia to explore how different memory systems are affected by hippocampal damage. Using experimental recognition tasks involving words, faces, and topographical stimuli, the researchers assessed the contributions of recollection and familiarity processes. One patient with selective right hippocampal damage showed preserved verbal and face recognition but impaired topographical memory, while another patient with broader bilateral damage demonstrated global deficits. The findings suggest that different regions within the medial temporal lobe contribute selectively to memory for different kinds of material and that face recognition may rely more heavily on regions outside the hippocampus.
